Previous work has identified where Antarctic Dry Valley streams are reshaping the land, but it stops at location. It does not say why. This project attributes two decades of measured stream-channel change in the McMurdo Dry Valleys, the largest ice-free region of Antarctica, to its climate drivers. The valleys' ephemeral meltwater streams are an early, direct measure of a polar desert responding to warming. Three elevation epochs cover the valleys: a 2001 NASA airborne lidar DEM, a 2014 airborne lidar DEM, and 2021-23 REMA satellite DEMs co-registered to the airborne lidar and ICESat-2 control. Differencing them shows where the land moved. The project then asks what moved it.

Objective 1 tests whether melt energy predicts a stream's rate of channel change better than water volume alone after controlling for substrate (H1). The hypothesis fails if discharge alone predicts channel migration as well as melt energy does, or if substrate class alone absorbs the explanatory power. Objective 2 classifies change patches along the stream corridor by process type and tests whether each process carries its own driver fingerprint (H2). Bank thaw should track summer thaw and ground-ice class, and channel shift should track peak discharge. Objective 3 replaces the single valley-wide noise value with a calibrated per-pixel detection threshold built from slope, aspect, and sensor pair, verified on ground that did not change.

The methods are machine-learning segmentation of stream channels from high-resolution terrain (a U-Net trained on hand-digitized channel labels), DEM differencing gated by the per-pixel detection threshold, and driver attribution with hierarchical regression and random forest models validated by leave-one-stream-out cross-validation. Driver histories combine McMurdo Dry Valleys LTER station records with ERA5 and AMPS reanalysis, so every stream is covered, gauged or not.

The work is relevant to NASA's Earth Science Division and spans three of its research spheres, the Cryosphere, the Hydrosphere, and the Geosphere. It turns an underused 2001 NASA airborne lidar baseline, REMA satellite DEMs, and ICESat-2 control into an uncertainty-calibrated record of climate-driven surface change, and the resulting methodology applies to any elevation-differencing problem, including records from NASA missions such as ICESat-2 and NISAR. Knowing the driver turns monitoring into forecasting, in the Dry Valleys and in any polar region with repeat elevation data. All inputs are public. All products, including change rasters, process-classified maps, per-stream rate tables, uncertainty layers, and code, will be released open-access with DOIs, consistent with NASA open-science practice.
