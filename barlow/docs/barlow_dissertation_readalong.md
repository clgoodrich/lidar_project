## Front matter & Abstract (PDF pp. 1–16)
**In one breath:** Standard front matter plus a dense two-page abstract that is actually the whole dissertation in miniature — read the abstract carefully, skip the rest.
Skip pp. 1–6 (title page, copyright, dedication, acknowledgments) and skim pp. 9–16 (TOC, list of tables, list of figures) only to orient yourself — though the TOC on pp. 9–11 is worth 60 seconds because it shows the three-paper structure: Ch. 4 (U-Net stream detection, Taylor Valley), Ch. 5 (DEM co-registration/uncertainty), Ch. 6 (scale the U-Net to all valleys), Ch. 7 (change detection using Ch. 6's boundaries and Ch. 5's error model). The abstract (pp. 7–8) makes four claims in plain English: (1) she trained a U-Net on DEM-derived rasters (elevation, slope, aspect, curvature, flow accumulation) to segment ephemeral stream *boundaries* — full channel polygons, not centerlines — for 2001, 2014, and 2021–23, and found elevation/slope/aspect were the most informative inputs; (2) satellite photogrammetric DEMs (REMA, from WorldView stereo pairs) are good enough for change detection *if* you co-register them to lidar with point-to-plane ICP, and their vertical errors are heavy-tailed (Laplacian, not Gaussian), so she uses NMAD (Normalized Median Absolute Deviation — a robust stand-in for standard deviation) instead of RMSE; (3) DEM differencing masked to the stream polygons over two decades shows real but patchy change — Denton Hills most erosional, coastal Taylor Valley depositional, Wright/Victoria roughly balanced; (4) where three epochs exist (Taylor Valley), some streams show *accelerating* change. If any of that sentence-by-sentence decode sticks, you can read the rest of the dissertation as elaboration.

## Chapter 1 — Introduction (PDF pp. 17–30)
**The chapter in one breath:** "The most stable landscape on Earth is starting to move, its ephemeral meltwater streams are the tell, nobody can map them at scale — so I'll segment them with a U-Net and difference DEMs inside the resulting polygons."

### Chapter opening (pp. 17–20)
**What's happening:** Four pages of motivation before any numbered section. The pitch: the McMurdo Dry Valleys (MDVs) — Antarctica's largest ice-free area — have been geomorphically dead for as long as anyone has watched, which makes them a clean climatic baseline; recent warming, glacial melt, and permafrost thaw are waking them up, and the streams fed by glacier melt are where that change concentrates. She then names the technical gap (no way to semi-automate stream boundary segmentation across the whole MDV system for decadal change detection) and the fix (U-Net segmentation feeding change detection).
**Key terms:** MDVs — McMurdo Dry Valleys, the study area; hyper-arid polar desert — near-zero precipitation, all water arrives as glacier melt; ephemeral streams — channels that flow only during the short summer melt window (weeks), dry the rest of the year; fluvial geomorphic change — erosion/deposition reshaping of channels by flowing water; energy-limited (vs. precipitation-limited) — melt volume is set by the surface energy balance (solar radiation, temperature), not by rainfall, so tiny energy shifts produce outsized hydrologic response.
**Don't get lost:** These pages are heavily citation-padded and repeat the same argument three times with different references. The paragraph on p. 18 starting "In glaciated regions of the MDVs..." is just saying: streams are fed only by glacier melt, so channel change is a proxy for melt and permafrost thaw. Pages 18–19 ("While the MDVs may currently still be relatively stable..." and "The MDV system is characteristically energy-limited...") both make the identical "canary in the coal mine" point — MDV change may foreshadow continent-wide change. The last paragraph of p. 19 into p. 20 is the one that matters: change detection needs the channel delineated *first*, delineation doesn't scale over variable terrain and ephemeral flow, and U-Net is the proposed answer. Note "Barlow et al., 2022" citations — that's the author's own prior paper (Remote Sensing 14(1):234), which becomes Chapter 4.
**Takeaway:** Stream-channel change is the most sensitive readable signal of climate-driven destabilization in the MDVs, and the blocker to reading it at scale is automated stream *boundary* mapping.

### 1.1 Historical Background (pp. 20–24)
**What's happening:** A quick history of MDV observation, from a 1603 sighting and the Hondius map through the heroic-age expeditions to modern instrument records. The narrative arc she wants you to absorb: every survey from 1903 to 2001 found the glaciers in equilibrium — then a 2001 extreme warming event broke the pattern and kicked off a decade of geomorphic change. The second half (pp. 23–24) inventories the datasets that history left behind, which are exactly the ones she'll use.
**Key terms:** LTER — Long-Term Ecological Research project (since 1992; monitors 16 MDV streams with gaging, total station, and terrestrial lidar); TLS — terrestrial lidar scanning; ICESat — NASA's Ice, Cloud, and land Elevation Satellite (the 2001 airborne lidar was flown to calibrate it); NCALM — National Center for Airborne Laser Mapping (flew the 2014 lidar).
**Don't get lost:** Pages 20–22 (Castilla, Hondius, Ross, Scott, Terra Nova, NZAP glacier monitoring) are pleasant but skippable color; p. 21 and p. 23 are mostly figure (the Hondius map, Fig. 1.1; the 1956 aerial photo, Fig. 1.2). Slow down at two spots. First, the end of p. 22: the 2001 extreme warming event is the pivot the whole dissertation hangs on — it's why 2001 vs. 2014 vs. 2021–23 comparisons are interesting. Second, pp. 23–24: the 2001 NASA/NSF/USGS airborne lidar and the 2014 NCALM lidar are the two epochs differenced in Chapters 5–7 — remember them. Of the recent-studies list on p. 24, the only ones that matter later are Fountain et al. (2014) and Levy et al. (2018), the prior whole-MDV change-detection work she extends; her critique of all prior work is "small segments of well-known streams, or coarse landscape-scale trends — nothing stream-scale and system-wide."
**Takeaway:** A century of "everything's in equilibrium" ended around 2001, and two airborne lidar campaigns (2001, 2014) bracket the shift — they are the backbone data of this dissertation.

### 1.2 Stream Channel Detection (pp. 24–25)
**What's happening:** The methods-gap argument for the segmentation half of the work. She dismisses the two standard approaches: flow-accumulation routing (assumes precipitation-driven drainage, so it misroutes channels near glacier margins where melt actually enters the system) and centerline-plus-cross-section bank estimation (needs manual cleanup, misses subtle banks between cross-sections, doesn't scale).
**Don't get lost:** This section is short and clean; the one framing to hold onto is *boundaries vs. centerlines*. You need full channel polygons — the whole active width — to mask DEM differencing, and existing methods give you lines. The p. 25 paragraph about "low-amplitude inflection points and gradual planform curvature" is just saying: MDV channels are so shallow and narrow that interpolating banks between spaced cross-sections misses them.
**Takeaway:** Existing stream extraction gives centerlines under rainfall assumptions; change detection needs full boundary polygons in a melt-driven landscape, and nothing off the shelf does that.

### 1.3 Geomorphological Change Detection (pp. 25–26)
**What's happening:** The methods-gap argument for the change-detection half. Standard practice — difference multi-epoch DEMs to map erosion and deposition — is fine in principle, but in the MDVs the signal is tiny and the DEM errors are not, so uncertainty handling decides what you can legitimately call "change."
**Key terms:** incision / aggradation — channel bed cutting down / building up; avulsion — a channel abruptly abandoning its course and jumping to a new one; lateral migration — the channel sweeping sideways across the valley floor.
**Don't get lost:** One page, two ideas. The p. 25 process list (incision, aggradation, narrowing/widening, migration, avulsion) is the vocabulary Chapter 7's results are described in — worth 30 seconds. The p. 26 paragraph on resolution and multi-epoch uncertainty is the seed of all of Chapter 5.
**Takeaway:** DEM differencing works here only if vertical uncertainty is characterized carefully enough to separate subtle real change from noise — that's why an entire chapter (5) is spent on error modeling.

### 1.4 Summary (pp. 26–28)
**What's happening:** A recap that re-states pp. 17–26 in compressed form, then (top of p. 28) pivots to a first plain statement of the contribution: a segmented stream boundary dataset covering the 2001/2014 lidar extents, used to find where geomorphic change is happening and accelerating.
**Don't get lost:** Pages 26–27 contain nothing you haven't already read — genuinely skimmable. Two new nuggets: mid-p. 27 concedes that existing repeat data (TLS, total station) covers only small segments of major channels, and the final paragraph of p. 27 introduces repeat WorldView-derived DEMs as the thing that makes broad-scale *repeat* analysis (and hence acceleration detection) possible going forward — that's the REMA data of Chapters 5 and 7. The p. 28 paragraph ("My main contribution is...") is the crispest statement of the whole project so far; read that one closely.
**Takeaway:** The deliverable is a multi-valley stream boundary dataset plus a change/acceleration framework, with satellite DEMs extending monitoring beyond the two lidar snapshots.

### 1.5 Objectives and Contributions (pp. 28–29)
**What's happening:** The three numbered objectives the rest of the guide will keep referring back to. Verbatim in spirit:
1. **Detect** — build a U-Net stream-boundary detection approach on 2001/2014 lidar DEMs and 2021–23 WorldView-derived DEMs, yielding a pre-trained model and the first comprehensive database of stream locations across the MDVs (delivered in Ch. 6, prototyped in Ch. 4).
2. **Quantify** — compute fluvial geomorphic rates of change across the full MDV extent via DEM differencing (Ch. 7, enabled by Ch. 5's error work).
3. **Accelerate** — detect acceleration of change using the lidar epochs plus 2021–23 WorldView DEMs along select Taylor Valley streams, i.e., the three-epoch analysis (Ch. 7).
**Don't get lost:** Nothing dense here; just note the scope asymmetry baked into objective 3 — acceleration (three epochs) is only assessed where 2021–23 REMA coverage exists, essentially Taylor Valley, not the whole MDV system.
**Takeaway:** Detect boundaries (1), quantify change rates system-wide (2), detect acceleration where three epochs exist (3) — map these onto Chapters 6, 7, and 7 respectively, with 4 and 5 as the methodological groundwork.

### 1.6 Dissertation Structure (pp. 29–30)
**What's happening:** A bulleted chapter roadmap. Useful mostly as confirmation of the dependency chain: Ch. 2 technical background, Ch. 3 study area and datasets, Ch. 4 U-Net proof-of-concept in Taylor Valley on 2014 lidar features (her published 2022 paper), Ch. 5 REMA-to-lidar co-registration and error characterization, Ch. 6 the multi-epoch multi-valley boundary dataset, Ch. 7 the change/acceleration analysis, Ch. 8 conclusions and future work.
**Don't get lost:** One subtlety worth catching in the bullets: Ch. 4 uses "topographic *and reflectance*" features (lidar intensity was an input in the prototype), while Ch. 6 says "DEM-derived terrain attributes" only — intensity gets dropped when she moves to satellite DEMs, which have no lidar intensity. That quiet input change explains differences you'll see between the Ch. 4 and Ch. 6 feature-importance tables.
**Takeaway:** Read the dissertation as prototype (4) → error budget (5) → production dataset (6) → science result (7); Chapters 2–3 are reference material you can dip into as needed.

## Chapter 2 — Technical Background (PDF pp. 31–47)
**The chapter in one breath:** The methods toolbox — polar fluvial process context, lidar and satellite-stereo DEM generation, point-to-plane ICP co-registration, why classical stream-extraction methods fail in the Dry Valleys, U-Net as the replacement, and DEM-of-Difference for volumetric change.
As a GIS/lidar professional you can skim the lidar fundamentals (pp. 33–35) and the U-Net architecture walkthrough (pp. 44–46) — both are textbook-level. You must actually read Point-to-Plane ICP (pp. 37–39) and Stream Channel Detection Methods (pp. 39–43): the first sets up the co-registration/error logic every change-detection result in later chapters depends on, and the second is the argument for why the dissertation exists (why she needed U-Net at all).

### Fluvial Geomorphology of Polar Regions (p. 32)
**What's happening:** One page arguing that cold-desert channels are unusually sensitive instruments: with permafrost, scarce sediment, and brief meltwater pulses, even small hydrologic shifts change channel form. Freeze–thaw cycling, active-layer deepening, and permafrost degradation alter sediment cohesion and mobility, so climate directly modulates how erodible the landscape is.
**Key terms:** active layer — the shallow surface zone above permafrost that thaws each summer; its deepening frees more sediment for transport.
**Don't get lost:** It's a single page of citation-dense framing (Knighton, Charlton, Lane). You need one idea from it: MDV channels respond to small forcings, which is why the dissertation bothers detecting small changes at all.
**Takeaway:** MDV stream channels are climate-sensitive because permafrost and meltwater timing control sediment mobility — the justification for measuring subtle geomorphic change.

### Remote Sensing Principles > Light Detection and Ranging (pp. 33–35)
**What's happening:** Standard airborne lidar (ALS) primer: pulse time-of-flight ranging, GNSS+IMU georeferencing, footprint size from beam divergence and altitude, multiple returns, intensity, and wavelength choice (1064 nm absorbed by water vs 532 nm for bathymetry). Ends with error sources — GNSS/IMU uncertainty, multipath, flight-line striping — and the point that lidar repeat surveys in the MDVs are too costly, motivating satellite stereo as the multi-epoch source.
**Key terms:** None you don't already know.
**Don't get lost:** The range equation on p. 33 (ρ = cτ/2n) is just "distance = speed × half the round-trip time, corrected for the medium's refractive index" — nothing more. Skim to the last paragraph of p. 35: lidar is the accurate-but-rare baseline epoch; stereophotogrammetry provides the repeats. That asymmetry drives the whole multi-source design.
**Takeaway:** Lidar is the gold-standard reference surface, but repeat coverage of Antarctica comes from satellite stereo, so the two must be fused.

### Remote Sensing Principles > Stereophotogrammetry (pp. 35–37)
**What's happening:** How DEMs come out of overlapping satellite images. Classical parallax/similar-triangle geometry doesn't apply to pushbroom sensors (line-by-line acquisition, continuously varying orientation, no fixed air base), so WorldView-class imagery uses Rational Polynomial Coefficients to map ground-to-image geometry per pixel. Matching fails in low-texture, shadowed, or repetitive terrain, and small stereo convergence angles amplify parallax error into elevation error. SETSM (p. 37) is the coarse-to-fine matching algorithm behind the REMA Antarctic DEMs.
**Key terms:** pushbroom sensor — satellite camera that images one line at a time as it orbits, so there is no single exposure moment; RPCs (Rational Polynomial Coefficients) — vendor-supplied rational-polynomial fit approximating the sensor model, letting you intersect viewing rays without the physical camera model; convergence angle — angle between the two viewing directions at a ground point; small angle = weak geometry = noisy heights; SETSM — Surface Extraction from TIN-based Search-Space Minimization, the automated DEM extractor used for REMA; REMA — Reference Elevation Model of Antarctica, the continent-wide DEM product from WorldView/GeoEye stereo.
**Don't get lost:** No equations here. The load-bearing sentences are the last ones on p. 37: stereo DEMs carry unavoidable residual error from geometry, texture, and sensor-model limits — therefore co-registration is mandatory before differencing. That "because of these uncertainties" hand-off is the whole reason the next section exists.
**Takeaway:** REMA-style stereo DEMs are good but never error-free, so any change detection stands or falls on the co-registration step that follows.

### Remote Sensing Principles > Point-to-Plane ICP (pp. 37–39)
**What's happening:** The co-registration workhorse. ICP iteratively finds the rigid rotation+translation aligning two point clouds; the point-to-plane variant minimizes distance from a source point to the tangent plane at its nearest neighbor rather than point-to-point distance. Then the crucial error discussion: a small residual horizontal shift between DEMs produces slope- and aspect-dependent vertical errors that mimic erosion/deposition — following a predictable sinusoidal-with-aspect pattern (Nuth & Kääb) — and even good registration isn't spatially uniform, sometimes requiring localized/subsectioned co-registration.
**Key terms:** ICP (Iterative Closest Point) — iterative rigid-body alignment of two point clouds by minimizing distances between corresponding points; point-to-plane ICP — variant minimizing distance to the local tangent plane, using surface normals; Nuth & Kääb pattern — the sinusoidal relationship between elevation-difference error and terrain aspect that diagnoses a horizontal DEM shift.
**Don't get lost:** The ICP mechanics on pp. 37–38 boil down to: solve a linearized least-squares for rotation+translation, repeat until convergence. Retain exactly two things: (1) point-to-plane converges faster and handles smooth, gently varying topography better than point-to-point because sliding along a planar surface isn't penalized — exactly the MDV valley-floor situation; (2) the misregistration signature (p. 38, Figure 2.1 on p. 39) — horizontal shift × slope × aspect = fake elevation change in a sinusoidal aspect pattern. Page 39 is essentially figure-only (Figure 2.1 plus a closing sentence). Later chapters will treat residual misalignment as an error term; this is where that logic is established.
**Takeaway:** Point-to-plane ICP is chosen because it converges well on smooth polar terrain, and the Nuth & Kääb slope/aspect error pattern is the fingerprint you check to know whether "change" is real or a registration artifact.

### Stream Channel Detection Methods (pp. 39–43)
**What's happening:** A systematic teardown of every conventional channel-mapping approach as applied to the MDVs. Manual delineation doesn't scale or reproduce. Spectral/water-index methods (NDWI) need standing water — MDV channels are dry most of the year, and water masks find water surfaces, not geomorphic banks. Flow-accumulation methods (D8, D-Infinity) extract centerlines, not boundaries; require sink-filling/smoothing that erases the very microtopography shallow channels live in; and assume precipitation-uniform runoff, wrong for melt-dominated, glacier-fed hydrology. Cross-section bank detection (slope inflection points along the centerline) assumes clear slope breaks — absent in shallow braided MDV channels — inherits all centerline errors, and doesn't scale across multiple valleys.
**Key terms:** D8 — flow routing assigning each pixel one downslope flow direction; simple, best for small basins; D-Infinity — multiple-flow-direction routing splitting flow between up to two neighbors; can over-disperse; NDWI — Normalized Difference Water Index, a spectral band ratio highlighting open water; cross-section method — placing transects perpendicular to a centerline and picking banks at slope inflections, then interpolating between transects.
**Don't get lost:** This section is an argument, not a method — read it as a checklist of assumptions each method makes (water present; slope break exists; runoff spatially uniform; centerline correct) and note that the MDVs violate every one. The D8/D-Infinity material (pp. 40–41) and cross-section critique (pp. 41–42) each end with an MDV-specific failure sentence — those are the parts later chapters cite implicitly. The closing paragraph (pp. 42–43) is the pivot sentence to deep learning: segment full channel boundaries directly, over large areas, no centerline interpolation.
**Takeaway:** Every traditional method extracts centerlines or water, assumes conditions the MDVs lack, and can't scale — which is the motivation for boundary segmentation with a CNN.

### Convolutional Neural Networks > U-Net for Remote Sensing Applications (pp. 43–44)
**What's happening:** Why U-Net specifically: encoder-decoder semantic segmentation that performs well with small training sets — the deciding factor, since labeled data in remote sensing (and Antarctica especially) is expensive. Cites successful RS uses (roads, building footprints, land cover).
**Key terms:** semantic segmentation — per-pixel classification of an image (you likely know this; it's defined here on p. 43).
**Don't get lost:** Skim. The one claim to retain (p. 44): U-Net's skip connections, augmentation, and multi-resolution design let it work with far fewer training samples than other encoder-decoders — that's what makes hand-labeling a tractable path in later chapters.
**Takeaway:** U-Net is picked for its small-training-set performance, which is the binding constraint in a region with no existing labels.

### Convolutional Neural Networks > U-Net Architecture (pp. 44–46)
**What's happening:** The standard walkthrough: contracting path (conv + max-pool) captures context but loses spatial resolution; expanding path (transposed convolutions) recovers it; skip connections concatenate encoder features into the decoder for precise localization. Figure 2.2 (p. 46 — figure-only page apart from the section heading that follows) is the classic Ronneberger diagram.
**Key terms:** None new for this reader.
**Don't get lost:** Textbook material — skim, except one sentence near the bottom of p. 45: U-Net "has yet to be trained on ephemeral streams that often lack water and use topographic attributes rather than RGB values." That is the dissertation's stated novelty claim — inputs are terrain derivatives, not imagery. Everything else on pp. 44–46 you already know.
**Takeaway:** Standard U-Net, but the novel move is feeding it topographic attributes instead of RGB to segment dry, ephemeral channels.

### Volumetric Change Detection (pp. 46–47)
**What's happening:** DEM of Difference: subtract two epochs' elevation models (∆E = E1 − E2, Eq. 2.1), multiply by cell area to get volume, sum erosion and deposition within a watershed for a net sediment budget, and divide total change by elapsed time (Eq. 2.2) to get a rate she calls differential geomorphic change (DGC).
**Key terms:** DoD (DEM of Difference) — the per-pixel elevation-difference raster between two epochs; sediment budget — net volume from summing erosion (negative) and deposition (positive) cells over a watershed; DGC (differential geomorphic change) — total elevation change divided by time between epochs, i.e., a change rate.
**Don't get lost:** Half a page, two trivially simple equations — Eq. 2.1 is a subtraction and Eq. 2.2 is a division by time. The subtlety isn't here; it's upstream: a DoD is only as good as the co-registration (Section 2.2.3) that preceded it. Note the section stops short of thresholding/uncertainty (no level-of-detection treatment here) — expect the error handling in later chapters, built on the ICP/Nuth-&-Kääb material.
**Takeaway:** DoD turns aligned DEM pairs into erosion/deposition volumes and rates — mechanically trivial, entirely dependent on the registration quality established earlier in the chapter.

## Chapter 3 — Study Area & Datasets (PDF pp. 48–62)
**The chapter in one breath:** The McMurdo Dry Valleys are Antarctica's largest ice-free region, where all streamflow comes from glacier melt and the ground is permafrost — and the dissertation will measure stream change there using three elevation snapshots: 2001 lidar, 2014 lidar, and 2021–23 satellite DEMs.
This is a reference chapter — read it once for the mental map (place names, the three microclimate zones, the three datasets), then treat the valley-by-valley details as a lookup table you'll return to when later chapters name a specific glacier or stream.

### Regional Setting (pp. 48–49)
**What's happening:** The MDVs (~5000 km²) sit between the East Antarctic Ice Sheet and the Ross Sea; the Transantarctic Mountains block ice flow and create a rain shadow, keeping the valleys ice-free and geomorphically stable for ~10 million years. Since 2002, warming events have started to destabilize this — glacier thinning, rising lakes, thermokarst subsidence — which is the motivation for the whole dissertation. Page 49 lays out the four valley systems, north to south: Victoria (653 km²; includes Victoria, Barwick, McKelvey, Balham, Bull Pass), Wright (485 km²), Taylor (630 km², the best-studied), and Denton Hills (753 km²; includes Ward, Hidden, Miers, Garwood).
**Key terms:** thermokarst — irregular subsidence/collapse of the ground surface caused by melting of ground ice; rain shadow — mountains intercept moisture so the leeward side stays dry.
**Don't get lost:** Memorize the four valley-system names and their north-to-south order from p.49 — every result in Chapters 5–7 is reported per valley. Keep Figure 3.1 (the map, p.50) bookmarked; it's the only overview map of these place names. The km² figures and sub-valley lists are lookup material, not memorization material.
**Takeaway:** Four valley systems — Victoria, Wright, Taylor, Denton Hills — form a stable-for-10-million-years landscape that is now "on the threshold of change," and monitoring that change is the dissertation's job.

### Climate (pp. 50–51)
**What's happening:** The MDVs get under 10 cm/yr of snow, most of which sublimates (goes straight from ice to vapor), so liquid precipitation is essentially absent — meltwater production is controlled by temperature, not by how much falls from the sky. Mean annual air temperature is −18 to −22°C; in summer (November–February) valley bottoms oscillate around the melting point and warming spikes can hit +10°C. Conditions get wetter toward the coast and drier inland/upland, setting up strong spatial gradients in melt.
**Key terms:** sublimation — ice converting directly to vapor without melting; the dominant loss process for MDV snow.
**Don't get lost:** Note that "summer" here means November–February — later chapters' talk of "melt seasons" and survey timing all assumes this austral calendar. Page 50 (top) is mostly Figure 3.1; the climate text starts mid-page.
**Takeaway:** Melt — and therefore all stream activity — is temperature-limited, so small warming shifts translate directly into geomorphic change.

### Microclimate Zones (pp. 51–52)
**What's happening:** The MDVs are conventionally split into three zones: the Coastal Thaw Zone (CTZ — warm, moist, coastal margins and lower valley floors), the Inland Mixing Zone (IMZ — intermediate elevations, cooler and drier), and the Upland Stable Zone (USZ — coldest and driest, inland uplands and glacier surfaces). Their extents vary by valley.
**Key terms:** CTZ / IMZ / USZ — the three-letter microclimate zones (Coastal Thaw, Inland Mixing, Upland Stable), ordered from most to least melt-prone.
**Don't get lost:** These three acronyms are used constantly for the rest of the document — glaciers, permafrost types, and change rates all get sorted by zone. If you absorb only one framework from Chapter 3, make it CTZ > IMZ > USZ as a wetness/activity gradient.
**Takeaway:** CTZ/IMZ/USZ is the spatial vocabulary the dissertation uses to explain why some valleys change fast and others barely change at all.

### Cryosphere (pp. 52–56)
**What's happening:** Umbrella section covering the two frozen reservoirs — glaciers (3.3.1) and permafrost (3.3.2) — that supply and constrain all the water in the system. The two subsections below carry the content.
**Don't get lost:** The 3.3 heading on p.52 is just a container; go straight into the glacier subsection.
**Takeaway:** Nearly all MDV water is locked in glaciers and ground ice, so the cryosphere sections explain both where meltwater comes from and how erodible the ground it flows over is.

### Glaciers (pp. 52–53)
**What's happening:** Virtually all hydrologic activity originates as glacier melt — the system is energy-limited, not precipitation-limited, so glaciers respond fast to tiny temperature/radiation shifts. Melt is greatest in low, coastal settings: CTZ alpine glaciers (Miers, Canada, Commonwealth, Garwood) are thinning fast; IMZ outlet glaciers (Taylor, Wright Lower) are transitional, with Wright Lower thinning while Taylor's ablation zone stays near equilibrium (losing mass by sublimation); cold inland/upland glaciers (Victoria system) mostly sublimate and produce little melt.
**Key terms:** ablation zone — the lower part of a glacier where mass is lost (by melt or sublimation); outlet glacier — a glacier draining an ice sheet through a valley, vs. a locally-sourced alpine glacier.
**Don't get lost:** The named glaciers on pp.52–53 (Canada, Commonwealth, Taylor, Garwood, Miers, Wright Lower) recur as stream sources in the results chapters — skim now, look back later. The pattern to keep: melt intensity tracks the CTZ→USZ gradient.
**Takeaway:** Which glaciers melt (coastal CTZ ones) vs. which sublimate (inland ones) predicts where streams will be active and where change will concentrate.

### Permafrost (pp. 53–56)
**What's happening:** Permafrost extends 70–100 m deep, split between ice-cemented soil (~55%, frozen soil bound by ice) and dry-frozen soil (~43%, frozen but ice-poor). The active layer — the top slice that thaws each summer — runs ~30–75 cm, deeper near the coast, only ~0.10 m inland. The key mechanic: freeze–thaw cycles weaken ice-cemented banks, so ice-cemented ground fails fast and erodes dramatically, while dry-frozen ground barely budges. Distribution follows the zones: coastal CTZ valleys (Denton Hills ~98% ice-cemented, plus buried massive ice from the Ross Sea Drift) are the most change-prone; Taylor (~67% dry-frozen) and Wright (~87% dry-frozen) valley floors are intermediate; Victoria system is ~89% dry-frozen and most stable.
**Key terms:** active layer — the surface layer that thaws seasonally above permafrost; ice-cemented permafrost — soil bound together by pore ice, cohesive until it thaws, then prone to collapse; dry-frozen permafrost — frozen soil with little ice, so thaw changes little; massive buried ice — large relict ice bodies under sediment (here, left by the Ross Sea Drift glacial deposit).
**Don't get lost:** Pages 54 and 56 are full-page figures (Fig. 3.2 active-layer depth map; Fig. 3.3 permafrost-type map, both from Bockheim et al. 2007) — bookmark Fig. 3.3, since permafrost type is the single best predictor of which streams show change later. The percentage-by-valley recitation on p.55 is lookup material; the takeaway is the ranking: Denton Hills most erodible, Victoria system least.
**Takeaway:** Ice-cemented (coastal) vs. dry-frozen (inland) permafrost is what makes stream banks erodible or inert — it's the ground-truth explanation for the spatial change patterns in later chapters.

### Hydrosphere (pp. 56–60)
**What's happening:** "Stream" is defined broadly here (p.56): any meltwater-driven channel with a discernible topographic form, from named rivers to short glacier-fed streams — worth noting since the whole detection task hinges on this definition. Streams are ephemeral, narrow, incised into ice-cemented soil, and "flashy": small glacier-fed Taylor Valley streams run 10–25 L/s, the Onyx River (Antarctica's longest at ~32 km, Wright Lower Glacier → Lake Vanda) and Garwood River approach 1 m³/s mean with peaks over 10 m³/s, and interannual flow can swing by 3× to >1000× — some channels skip entire summers. Drainage architecture differs by valley: Taylor's streams feed closed-basin saline lakes, Wright is dominated by the Onyx flowing inland, southern coastal rivers (Garwood, Miers) discharge to the Ross Sea, and Victoria's channels flow only in exceptional years. Page 59's paragraph on hyporheic exchange explains reach-scale variability, and p.60 delivers the punchline: many channels have such subtle topographic signatures that traditional flow-accumulation extraction fails — hence the deep learning approach of later chapters.
**Key terms:** ephemeral stream — flows only part of the year (here, a few summer weeks); diel cycle — daily rise-and-fall of flow following the sun; hyporheic exchange — water moving between the channel and the saturated sediment beneath/beside it, so long channels "leak" flow into subsurface storage like a sponge; closed-basin lake — a lake with no outlet, so inflow raises its level.
**Don't get lost:** Page 59 is mostly Figure 3.4 (mapped streams, monitored streams in red, gages in green) — bookmark it as the stream-name gazetteer. The discharge numbers on pp.57–58 are context, not something to retain; the load-bearing sentence is on p.60, where subtle channel signatures justify the deep-learning method. Also note the explicit "stream" definition on p.56 — it defines what the model is supposed to detect.
**Takeaway:** MDV streams are faint, flashy, and hard to extract with conventional DEM hydrology — which is precisely the gap the dissertation's deep-learning boundary detection fills.

### Datasets (pp. 60–61)
**What's happening:** Three elevation datasets anchor everything: a 2001 airborne lidar survey, a 2014(–15) airborne lidar survey, and 2021–23 satellite stereo DEMs from REMA — used respectively to map stream boundaries, quantify multi-decadal elevation change, and test whether change is accelerating. Figure 3.5 (p.61) shows their footprints.
**Key terms:** REMA — Reference Elevation Model of Antarctica, a continent-wide DEM product built from stereo satellite imagery by the Polar Geospatial Center.
**Don't get lost:** Bookmark Figure 3.5 on p.61 (coverage map: 2001 NASA in red, 2014 NCALM in dark blue, 2014 REMA in light blue, 2021–23 REMA in green) — every change-detection result is bounded by where these footprints overlap. The three epochs — 2001, 2014, 2021–23 — are the temporal skeleton of the whole dissertation; fix them now.
**Takeaway:** Three epochs (2001 lidar, 2014 lidar, 2021–23 REMA) give two change intervals, which is what lets the dissertation ask not just "is the landscape changing?" but "is it accelerating?"

### Airborne Lidar Datasets (pp. 61–62)
**What's happening:** The 2001 NASA survey used the Airborne Topographic Mapper (ATM; 1550 and 532 nm, 10 kHz PRF), yielding a sparse one point per 2.7 m² over ~4,500 km². The 2014 NCALM/Portland State survey used a Teledyne Optech Titan multiwavelength sensor (1550/1064/532 nm, 50–300 kHz per channel) at 2–10 pts/m² (avg 4.7), with ~7 cm vertical / ~3 cm horizontal accuracy over ~3,600 km² — though the 532 nm channel saturated over bright snow/ice and wasn't used everywhere.
**Key terms:** PRF (pulse repetition frequency) — laser pulses per second, the main driver of point density; multiwavelength lidar — a sensor firing multiple laser colors (here green + two infrared), useful for distinguishing surface types.
**Don't get lost:** The specs to carry forward are the density asymmetry — ~0.37 pts/m² (2001) vs. 4.7 pts/m² (2014) — because it drives every methodological decision about resolution, uncertainty, and why the 2001 data is the limiting epoch. The wavelength/PRF details are lookup-only.
**Takeaway:** The 2014 survey is roughly an order of magnitude denser and far more accurate than 2001, so the older dataset sets the floor for what change is detectable.

### Satellite Derived DEMs (p. 62)
**What's happening:** REMA DEM strips, built by the Polar Geospatial Center from WorldView-1/2/3 stereo imagery (2009–2023) at 2 m resolution, supply the modern epoch: 2014 strips are used to develop the uncertainty-estimation method (against the 2014 lidar), while 2021–23 strips are the change-detection epoch. Caveat: the strips aren't registered to satellite altimetry, so co-registration is manual and time-intensive — which is why REMA-based change detection is limited to parts of Taylor Valley, with automation strategies deferred to later chapters.
**Key terms:** DEM strip — a raw, unadjusted elevation swath from one stereo image pair, needing co-registration before comparison; co-registration — aligning two DEMs so elevation differences reflect real change, not offset.
**Don't get lost:** Two things to lock in from p.62: (1) 2014 REMA = uncertainty calibration, 2021–23 REMA = actual change detection — don't conflate the roles; (2) the Taylor-Valley-only limitation is a stated scalability constraint, not an oversight, and it foreshadows the co-registration discussion later.
**Takeaway:** REMA extends the record to 2021–23 at 2 m resolution, but unregistered strips confine the third-epoch analysis to Taylor Valley for now.

## Chapter 4 — U-Net Stream Detection in Taylor Valley (PDF pp. 63–85)
**The chapter in one breath:** A ResNet-18/U-Net trained on 217 hand-labeled 300 m tiles of lidar-derived rasters segments dry, ephemeral stream boundaries across 770 km² of Taylor Valley — and a single-channel input (elevation or slope alone) beats every multi-channel combination, hitting F1 ≈ 0.94.

This is the published-paper chapter (Barlow, Zhu & Glennie 2022, *Remote Sensing* 14(1):234) with Taylor Valley as the proof-of-concept site. Chapter 6 scales the same approach to the full MDV system, and Chapter 7 uses the extracted stream extents to bound geomorphic change detection — so the design choices here (input channels, tile size, normalization) carry forward.

### Introduction and Related Work (pp. 63–68)
**What's happening:** She motivates why stream boundaries matter (width/cross-section drive hydrologic and sediment models) and why the MDVs break every existing method: flow is ephemeral, channels are narrow (~10–150 m), and "bankfull" indicators barely exist, so she collapses everything into one "stream area" class. She then spends pp. 66–67 dismantling the incumbent cross-section-based methods (De Rosa, McKean), which need manual cleanup wherever streams meander, braid, or intersect. U-Net is chosen because it segments without cross-sections, needs few training samples, and produces smooth connected features.
**Key terms:** *Bankfull width* — channel extent at the flow that just fills the channel before spilling onto the floodplain (recurs ~1.5–2 yr in temperate systems, far less often here). *Active channel width* — the narrower extent of recent/current flow; in MDV low-flow streams the two are indistinguishable, hence the single class (Figure 4.1, p. 65). *Braided stream* — multi-threaded channel splitting and rejoining around bars. *Ephemeral* — flows only during melt events.
**Don't get lost:** pp. 63–64 are standard lit-review citation stacking — skim. The substance is p. 65 (Figure 4.1, the bankfull-vs-active-channel cross-section diagram that justifies one class) and pp. 66–67, where Figure 4.2 (p. 67) shows the four geometry cases (straight, braided, meandering, intersecting) that make cross-section methods fail — this figure sets up the Results breakdown by geometry later. The claim to hold: nobody has done deep-learning stream-extent detection in hyper-arid regions at multi-basin scale before (p. 65).
**Takeaway:** Cross-section methods can't handle ephemeral, meandering/braided MDV streams, so she reframes boundary delineation as pixelwise segmentation with one merged stream class.

### Methods (pp. 69–75)
**What's happening:** The pipeline (flowchart, Figure 4.3, p. 69): rasterize 2014 NCALM lidar into four 1 m feature rasters, hand-digitize labels on 217 tiles, train a U-Net per feature combination, then predict the full valley on larger overlapping tiles.
**Don't get lost:** The whole Methods section is four subsections; each below. Figure 4.3 (p. 69) and Figure 4.4 (p. 69, location map) are orientation figures — glance and move on.
**Takeaway:** A conventional lidar-raster → hand-label → U-Net pipeline whose interesting decisions are per-tile normalization, single-feature inputs, and a 5× tile-size jump at inference.

### Methods > Data Preparation (pp. 69–72)
**What's happening:** 2014 NCALM airborne lidar (2.7 pts/m², ~560 km² ice-free Taylor Valley) is interpolated (natural neighbor) to a 1 m DEM and a 1 m intensity raster; slope and MFD flow accumulation are derived in ArcGIS. Labels are manually digitized using those rasters plus simultaneous 5–20 cm aerial imagery and topographic profiles.
**Key terms:** *MFD (multi-flow direction) flow accumulation* — DEM-derived raster of upstream contributing area, spreading flow to multiple downslope neighbors rather than one. *Natural neighbor interpolation* — Voronoi-weighted gridding of the point cloud. *Inflection point* — the break in slope at the channel bank used as the primary boundary cue.
**Don't get lost:** The spec, scattered across pp. 70–71: **four candidate channels** (elevation, slope, intensity, flow accumulation, all 1 m); **217 hand-labeled 300×300 m tiles** covering ~1% of the study area, sited where LTER stream centerlines plus imagery confirm a stream exists with visibly distinguishable boundaries; **two classes** (stream / non-stream); **per-tile min–max normalization** to 8-bit unsigned — each tile is stretched by its own min/max, a deliberate local-contrast enhancement that becomes load-bearing at prediction time (p. 76). Watch the labeling caveats on p. 72: tiles where bank inflections are confusable with underlying strata/outcrops were *excluded*, so the model is trained (and can only be fairly judged) on well-expressed boundaries. Note the honest ambiguity admission — limited field data means labels themselves carry uncertainty. Figure 4.5 (p. 73) is a figure-only page showing what each channel looks like over a stream.
**Takeaway:** Four 1 m lidar rasters, 217 hand-labeled 300 m tiles (~1% coverage), binary classes, and per-tile min–max normalization — with the label set deliberately restricted to unambiguous boundaries.

### Methods > U-Net (pp. 73–74)
**What's happening:** A half-page boilerplate recap of encoder-decoder segmentation. Nothing architecture-specific to this study appears here — the actual implementation details (ResNet-18 encoder, PyTorch) land in Training.
**Don't get lost:** You can skip this subsection entirely; there is no custom architecture, no skip-connection modification, no channel-count discussion.
**Takeaway:** Stock U-Net; the novelty is in the inputs and application, not the network.

### Methods > Training (pp. 74–75)
**What's happening:** She trains on the open-source DroneDeploy landcover tool (PyTorch 1.1.0, pre-trained ResNet-18 encoder in a U-Net) on free Colab GPUs (K80/T4/P4/P100, 4–6 h total), once per feature combination.
**Don't get lost:** The training spec (p. 74): **50/20/30 train/val/test split of the 217 tiles**, with the test set *manually selected* (not random) to represent all stream geometries and balance classes — fine for her diagnostic goal, but note it when reading the test metrics. **Augmentation: three 90° rotations only.** **Hyperparameters: 200 epochs, batch 16, LR 1e-5, weight decay 1e-4**, tuned by eyeballing loss-curve shape — no loss function is named (the DroneDeploy default, cross-entropy), and there's no explicit class-imbalance handling (focal loss, weighting) despite stream pixels being the minority. Metrics (p. 75): precision, recall, F1 — no IoU. One sentence to read twice: she frames "precision is representative of stream segmentation performance, while recall represents the segmentation performance of non-stream regions" — a nonstandard gloss; just track P/R/F1 as usual.
**Takeaway:** Off-the-shelf DroneDeploy U-Net (ResNet-18), 50/20/30 split of 217 tiles, rotation-only augmentation, empirically eyeballed hyperparameters, P/R/F1 evaluation.

### Methods > Prediction (pp. 75–76)
**What's happening:** For full-valley inference she keeps the top four single-feature models but jumps tile size from 300 m to **852 overlapping 1500×1500 m tiles**; full-valley prediction takes ~15 min.
**Don't get lost:** This is the chapter's best practitioner gotcha (p. 76): at 300 m inference tiles, edges got classified "stream" and stream bottoms "non-stream" because a tile holding only a channel fragment lacks context. But the 5× tile means per-tile min–max normalization now stretches over a much wider value range, so subtle low-activity streams flatten out and can be missed — she names this trade-off explicitly. Train-at-300 m / predict-at-1500 m with per-tile normalization is a genuine train/test distribution shift; she manages it empirically rather than fixing the normalization scheme.
**Takeaway:** Inference tiles were enlarged 5× to give the model channel context, accepting a normalization-induced sensitivity loss on faint streams.

### Results (pp. 76–82)
**What's happening:** Single-feature models beat every 2–4 feature combination. Elevation and slope tie for best; intensity and flow accumulation trail. Performance is mapped spatially (coastal > inland), against microclimate zones and geology (glacial till > bedrock), and by stream geometry (meandering best, straight worst — the inverse of cross-section methods). Underprediction, especially of small tributaries, is the dominant error mode.
**Don't get lost:** The headline numbers live in **Table 4.1 (p. 77)**: elevation P/R/F1 = 0.94±0.05 / 0.95±0.04 / 0.94±0.04; slope = 0.96±0.03 / 0.93±0.04 / 0.94±0.04; intensity and flow-accum F1 ≈ 0.93–0.94 but with weaker precision (0.88–0.89). **Table 4.2 (p. 78)** is the multi-feature graveyard — every combination lands F1 0.83–0.92, all below the singles; skim it once to confirm the pattern, don't memorize. Also on p. 77: per-class asymmetry — average true-positive rate 0.71 vs true-negative 0.93, i.e., the glossy F1 rides on abundant easy non-stream pixels; underprediction dominates. Figure 4.6 (p. 79) maps F1 (0.81–0.99 coastal, down to 0.70 inland); Figure 4.7 (p. 80) overlays microclimate zones (CTZ/IMZ/USZ — coastal thaw, intermediate, upper stable zones) and geology; Figure 4.8 (p. 81) is the geometry boxplot (straight worst, meandering best); Figure 4.9 (p. 82, figure-only page) is the Commonwealth Stream visual comparison where slope overpredicts tributaries and elevation stays cleaner. Note test metrics come from that manually curated, unambiguous-boundary test set.
**Takeaway:** Elevation alone at F1 0.94 wins; adding channels hurts, geometry difficulty inverts the cross-section literature, and the real weakness is missed faint tributaries (TP rate 0.71).

### Discussion (pp. 83–84)
**What's happening:** She explains the results: intensity/flow-accum lack the bank-inflection information and adding dimensions with a tiny training set hampers convergence (though intensity might help in wetter regions where water reflectance stands out). Coastal streams do better because they're hydrologically more active with better-evolved channels; straight streams are shallow with faint slope breaks, while meandering streams have had time to carve distinct banks. Limitations: label quality, training restricted to well-expressed boundaries, no strata/outcrop disambiguation.
**Don't get lost:** The practical claim on p. 83: elevation or slope *alone* suffices, so you can skip computing the other derivatives entirely — directly relevant if you're building your own terrain-feature stacks. p. 84 sets up the dissertation arc: apply this to detect active (water/ice/snow) streams, and feed segmented extents into fluvial change detection — that's Chapters 6–7.
**Takeaway:** With ~150 training tiles, fewer input channels converge better, and terrain shape (not reflectance) is what encodes dry-stream boundaries.

### Conclusions (p. 85)
**What's happening:** One-page recap: automated boundary segmentation of ~10–150 m ephemeral streams over 770 km² from just elevation or slope, trained on ~1% of the area; elevation 0.94/0.95/0.94 P/R/F1, slope 0.96/0.93/0.94; meandering geometries F1 0.67–0.98; coastal beats inland, till beats bedrock.
**Don't get lost:** Nothing new — pure restatement. The forward pointer is that this dataset seeds the full-MDV hydrography of Chapter 6.
**Takeaway:** A minimal-input, small-label-budget U-Net delivers multi-basin dry-stream extents good enough to build the rest of the dissertation on.

## Chapter 5 — Co-Registering Satellite DEMs (REMA) to Lidar (PDF pp. 86–107)
**The chapter in one breath:** Barlow aligns 2014 REMA satellite DEMs to 2014 NCALM lidar with point-to-plane ICP, then dissects the residual error by slope, aspect, and stream vs. non-stream terrain to build the uncertainty budget that all later change detection will lean on.
This is the "can we trust free satellite DEMs?" chapter. The Dry Valleys change so slowly that DEM noise can dwarf real geomorphic signal, so before Chapter 7 can difference DEMs across decades, this chapter has to establish how big the error is after co-registration, where it lives on the landscape, and how to mask or budget for it.

### 5.1 Introduction (pp. 86–88)
**What's happening:** Standard motivation: field monitoring in Antarctica is impractical, satellite DEMs give coverage and repeat frequency, but the MDVs evolve on decadal-to-centennial timescales, so change signal is tiny. Stereo-derived DEMs are worst exactly where streams live — incised channels, steep banks, shadows, bad viewing geometry. The chapter's stated job (bottom of p. 88): assess REMA's suitability for change detection and build the uncertainty framework Chapter 7 depends on.
**Key terms:** REMA (Reference Elevation Model of Antarctica) — continent-wide DEM from WorldView-1/2/3 stereo pairs, down to 2 m resolution; DoD (DEM of Difference) — the pixel-wise subtraction of two DEMs used for change detection.
**Don't get lost:** This is a literature-heavy setup with no data yet. The one load-bearing idea is on p. 87: in slowly changing terrain, error on *stable* ground masquerades as change, so error characterization is not optional bookkeeping — it is the analysis.
**Takeaway:** REMA is the only practical multi-temporal elevation source for the MDVs, but its terrain-driven errors must be quantified before any DoD is believable.

### 5.2 Methods (p. 88)
**What's happening:** One-paragraph roadmap (p. 88): co-register REMA to lidar, find topographic biases worth masking, and assess alignment accuracy specifically within stream extents. The four subsections below do the work.
**Don't get lost:** Nothing here beyond the roadmap — read it in ten seconds and move on.
**Takeaway:** The method is co-register, then characterize what alignment could not fix.

### 5.2.1 Dataset Acquisition and Pre-processing (pp. 89–92)
**What's happening:** Two datasets: 2014 NCALM lidar (5–10 pts/m², the reference) and 2014 REMA 2 m DEMs (SETSM-derived from WorldView stereo). Candidate REMA strips were culled iteratively — GeoEye-based strips were dropped for cross-track striping, others for stereo-matching artifacts — leaving two image pairs, Y14A (WV2/WV3, 2015-01-18) and Y14B (WV1/WV1, 2015-02-14). PGC quality metrics drove the initial screening; matchtag masks, reprojection to a Transantarctic Mountains CRS, and WGS84-ellipsoid-to-EGM2008-geoid conversion were applied — yet multi-meter vertical offsets still remained, which is why ICP is needed.
**Key terms:** REMA strips vs. mosaic — she uses individual strips (single acquisition dates), not the mosaic; SETSM — the stereo-extraction algorithm behind REMA; matchtag density (MMD/VAMD) — density of successfully matched stereo pixels, a proxy for DEM reliability; VA — percent of the tile with valid elevations; AC — stereo convergence angle (bigger is usually better, except in steep relief); AEHA — expected vertical accuracy from geometry alone; ASE — sun elevation at acquisition (low sun = long shadows = bad matching).
**Don't get lost:** Pages 90–91 define six PGC acronyms back to back — Table 5.1 (p. 91) is the decoder ring and the only place you need them: Y14A has AEHA 0.75 m, Y14B 1.01 m, both ~90% valid area. The selection bar was 85% valid area and sub-meter expected height accuracy (p. 90–91). Figure 5.1 (p. 91) maps lidar vs. REMA coverage. Note the punchline at the bottom of p. 91: even after datum and CRS corrections, several meters of vertical offset persisted — RPC geolocation error, per Howat et al.
**Takeaway:** Only two REMA strip pairs survived quality screening, and even the survivors arrive meters off vertically — co-registration is mandatory, not a refinement.

### 5.2.2 Co-registration (pp. 92–93)
**What's happening:** DEMs were split by watershed (ArcGIS Basin Tool) and each basin aligned independently to the lidar point cloud in CloudCompare: rough control points first, then fine alignment with point-to-plane ICP. Where post-alignment errors were biased, wide (σ > 0.5 m), or skewed, the basin was subdivided again along slope breaks and re-aligned, ending at units of roughly 0.5–6 km². Vertical error is defined simply as Δh = lidar minus REMA (Eq. 5.1, p. 93), with lidar downsampled to 2 m to match.
**Key terms:** Point-to-plane ICP — minimizes distance from points to the local tangent plane of the reference surface (via normals), rather than point-to-point distance; converges better on smooth terrain. As a practitioner you know this — the notable choice is doing it per-basin.
**Don't get lost:** The workflow is manual and iterative — align, inspect, subdivide, repeat — with explicit quality gates (mean centered on zero, σ ≤ 0.5 m, unskewed; p. 92). Eq. 5.1 (p. 93) fixes the sign convention: positive Δh means REMA sits *below* lidar. Keep that in mind when reading skewness signs in the Results.
**Takeaway:** Alignment is basin-by-basin point-to-plane ICP against lidar, iteratively subdivided until the error statistics pass — a labor-intensive but defensible recipe.

### 5.2.3 Topographic and Spatial Error Distribution Analysis (pp. 93–94)
**What's happening:** To find where REMA fails, errors are binned by lidar-derived slope (5° bins) and aspect (10° bins), and for each bin an exceedance probability curve — the fraction of errors exceeding a threshold, Eq. 5.2 (pp. 93–94) — is computed, using ±0.5 m as the "geomorphically significant" reference level. Bins that exceed too often get masked. Extra masks: 25 m buffers around glaciers and lakes, plus a shadow mask built from a hillshade cast at each image's actual sun position (shadowed cells = excluded).
**Key terms:** Exceedance probability — P(error > threshold) within a terrain bin; a per-terrain-class failure rate rather than an average error.
**Don't get lost:** Eq. 5.2 looks formal but is just a counting fraction: how often does this slope/aspect class blow past 0.5 m? The clever bit is deriving slope/aspect from the *lidar*, not from the error-prone REMA itself.
**Takeaway:** Rather than one global error number, the chapter builds a terrain-conditioned failure map that becomes a data-driven exclusion mask.

### 5.2.4 Co-registration Accuracy Assessment (pp. 94–95)
**What's happening:** Final accuracy is reported with RMSE, mean/median error, standard deviation, NMAD, skewness, and kurtosis. Histograms and Q–Q plots, with K–S statistics and AIC, test whether Gaussian or Laplacian better fits the errors (Laplace handles heavy tails). Crucially, the "stable terrain" assumption is introduced: non-stream areas in the MDVs don't change, so error measured there is pure error — and the stream/non-stream error relationship can be used to *predict* error inside stream corridors, where real change and artifact are otherwise entangled.
**Key terms:** NMAD — normalized median absolute deviation; read it as a robust standard deviation of elevation error, insensitive to outliers; K–S statistic — distance between empirical and fitted CDFs (smaller = better fit); AIC — model-comparison score penalizing complexity; Laplacian distribution — sharper peak, heavier tails than a Gaussian, the usual model for DEM error.
**Don't get lost:** The stable-region assumption (p. 95) is the methodological hinge of the whole dissertation's change-detection argument — if non-hydrologic terrain in the MDVs did change, everything downstream is biased. She cites prior MDV work to justify it; note it and carry it with you into Chapter 7.
**Takeaway:** Accuracy is judged with robust statistics on assumed-stable terrain, then extrapolated into stream corridors via a stream/non-stream error relationship.

### 5.3 Results (p. 95)
**What's happening:** Two-sentence framing paragraph on p. 95: quantify co-registration accuracy and the spatial pattern of error, in service of Chapter 7.
**Don't get lost:** Skip straight to the subsections.
**Takeaway:** The results establish both a headline accuracy number and where not to trust it.

### 5.3.1 Topographic Error Analysis (pp. 95–96)
**What's happening:** The exceedance analysis (Figure 5.2, p. 96) identifies two failure regimes: slopes steeper than ~50° at *any* aspect, and — more insidiously — south- to west-facing aspects (160–290°) at only moderate 15–40° slopes. Both exceed the 0.5 m error threshold too often and get masked via the piecewise binary mask function M(A,S) on p. 96.
**Don't get lost:** The mask equation on p. 96 renders as an ugly wall of case conditions — don't try to parse the algebra; it just encodes "exclude slopes ≥ ~48° everywhere, plus a wedge of south/west-facing moderate slopes." The physical story is illumination: SW-facing slopes carry the long shadows at Antarctic sun angles, and shadows kill stereo matching. Figure 5.2 caption confirms the ≥0.5 m threshold.
**Takeaway:** REMA error is not random — it is predictable from slope and aspect, so unfavorable terrain can be masked out before differencing.

### 5.3.2 Dataset Alignment Accuracy (pp. 97–103)
**What's happening:** This is the payoff section. Post-ICP errors are sharply peaked and heavy-tailed — clearly Laplacian, not Gaussian (K–S D of 0.01–0.04 for Laplace vs. 0.10–0.12 for Gaussian, p. 97) — which justifies NMAD as the headline metric. Stream corridors are measurably worse than stable ground, and a simple regression lets you predict stream error from non-stream error. Spatially, big errors cluster along steep channel walls and slope breaks.
**Don't get lost:** The headline accuracy numbers are in the text at the bottom of p. 97 (combined 2014 regions): mean error 0.01 m, σ 0.31 m, **NMAD 0.25 m**, RMSE 0.46 m, kurtosis 29.5 (that kurtosis is the heavy-tail smoking gun; a Gaussian would be 3). Table 5.2 (p. 99) is the one table to actually read — stream vs. non-stream: NMAD 0.28 m vs. 0.24 m, skewness −2.04 vs. −0.40, kurtosis 40 vs. 11. Eq. 5.3 (p. 99) is the transfer function: NMAD_stream = 0.972 × NMAD_non-stream + 0.034, i.e., streams run ~3 cm noisier, but note R² = 0.43 — only a moderate fit, which the Discussion owns up to. Page 98 is figure-only (Figure 5.3, histograms + Q–Q plots); p. 100 is essentially Figure 5.4 (the regression with 95% confidence/prediction bands); p. 101 is Figure 5.5 (observed vs. predicted stream NMAD maps); p. 103 is figure-only (Figure 5.6, the spatial error map). Skim the figures; the numbers above are the substance. Pages 101–102 add the spatial reading: worst errors sit at sharp slope breaks between channel beds and banks, and some clusters survive no matter how much you subdivide and re-run ICP.
**Takeaway:** After co-registration REMA is good to roughly ±0.25 m NMAD on stable ground and ~0.28 m in stream corridors — meaning only elevation changes comfortably above that (the level-of-detection Chapter 7 will formalize) can be called real.

### 5.4 Discussion (pp. 104–107)
**What's happening:** The discussion converts the results into practice. Slope/aspect control of error justifies terrain masking; Laplacian tails justify NMAD; stream corridors' skewed, heavy-tailed errors mean channel-bed elevations tend to be *overestimated*, which would masquerade as sediment deposition in a DoD — a serious trap in a landscape this slow. Residual error persists because stereo and lidar sample the surface fundamentally differently: small horizontal mismatches on steep, narrow, entrenched channels amplify into large apparent vertical offsets, and interpolation smooths channel banks differently in each dataset (pp. 105–106). Finer subdivision could help but risks overfitting the alignment and is unscalable without automation — flagged as future work, out of scope (p. 106).
**Don't get lost:** Three practitioner verdicts to extract: (1) same-year DEMs mean any apparent difference is error, not change (p. 105) — that's what licenses using this as an error budget; (2) the R² = 0.43 caveat (pp. 104–105) — she recommends using the upper 95% confidence/prediction interval of Eq. 5.3 for conservative stream-error estimates; (3) false-deposition bias in entrenched channels (p. 104) — when Chapter 7 shows deposition in a narrow channel, remember this warning.
**Takeaway:** Co-registered REMA is trustworthy for multi-decadal change detection on masked, moderate terrain at the ~0.25–0.3 m NMAD level, but deeply incised channels carry a known deposition-shaped bias that must be handled through masking and conservative uncertainty bounds.

## Chapter 6 — Scaling Up: Multi-Valley Stream Boundary Datasets (PDF pp. 108–129)
**The chapter in one breath:** Barlow retrains her U-Net stream-boundary model on a much larger multi-valley, multi-epoch label set and runs it wall-to-wall across the McMurdo Dry Valleys, producing the first high-resolution stream boundary polygon datasets for 2001, 2014, and 2021–23.
This is the production-scale sequel to chapter 4 (Barlow et al. 2022): Taylor Valley only, tile-wise normalization, DroneDeploy-based Python workflow become all-MDV coverage, per-landmass global normalization, and ArcGIS Pro's Deep Learning Toolbox — and the resulting stream masks are exactly what chapter 7 consumes for masked sediment-budget / geomorphic-change analysis.

### Introduction (pp. 108–109)
**What's happening:** Existing MDV stream maps are centerlines (LTER, LINZ) plus a few hand-drawn polygons around Lake Fryxell — spatially limited, outdated, and useless for masked sediment budgets. Ephemeral, mostly-dry channels defeat precipitation-driven and cross-section extraction methods, so she pitches DEM-derived topographic attributes + U-Net segmentation as the scalable alternative (p. 109).
**Takeaway:** The gap is multi-valley stream *polygons*, not centerlines, and deep-learning segmentation on terrain attributes is the only method that scales to it.

### Methods (p. 110)
**What's happening:** One-paragraph roadmap (p. 110): train the U-Net, then extract and filter stream boundaries MDV-wide. Details live in the four sub-sections below.
**Takeaway:** Same pipeline shape as chapter 4 — attributes in, binary mask out — but every stage is re-engineered for scale.

### Methods › Dataset Pre-Processing (pp. 110–112)
**What's happening:** Four DEM sources: 2001 NASA ATM lidar, 2014 NCALM lidar, and 2 m REMA DEMs from 2014 and 2021–23 (WorldView/GeoEye stereo). Table 6.1 (p. 111) specs them — 2001 lidar is 1 pt/2.7 m² with ±0.3 m/±0.2 m accuracy vs 2014's 4.7 pts/m² and ±3 cm/±7 cm. Six input attributes are derived in ArcGIS Pro with 3×3 kernels: elevation, slope, aspect, profile curvature, planform curvature, plus D-infinity flow accumulation.
**Key terms:** REMA (Reference Elevation Model of Antarctica, 2 m photogrammetric DEM); D-infinity flow accumulation (Tarboton's dispersive-flow routing).
**Don't get lost:** The load-bearing changes vs ch. 4 are on pp. 110–112: (1) everything is reprojected to WGS84 USGS Transantarctic Mountains with EGM2008 orthometric heights; (2) 2014 REMA test tiles are co-registered to the 2014 NCALM lidar per chapter 5, so cross-sensor accuracy comparison is legitimate; (3) normalization switches from Barlow et al. 2022's tile-wise scaling to global min-max per connected landmass (p. 112) — her stated fix for tile-edge discontinuities in the predicted boundaries; (4) lidar DEMs are natural-neighbor interpolated at 1 m.
**Takeaway:** Global (not tile-wise) normalization and cross-sensor co-registration are the two pre-processing decisions that make multi-valley, multi-epoch prediction coherent.

### Methods › Training Dataset Preparation (pp. 112–115)
**What's happening:** She scales labels from Taylor-only (217 in ch. 4) to 616 unique stream locations yielding 1,274 300×300 m tiles: 601 from 2014 NCALM, 528 from 2001 NASA, 145 from 2014 REMA (p. 112, Table 6.2 on p. 114). Sites are deliberately reused across epochs so the model sees subtle boundary shifts between dates/resolutions. Labels are still manually delineated two-class ("stream"/"non-stream") masks drawn against hillshade/elevation/slope/aspect/curvature.
**Don't get lost:** Table 6.2 (p. 114) breaks samples by valley group and landform (streams, waterbodies, glaciers as explicit distractor content). All inputs are resampled to a common 1 m grid so narrow channels in the high-res data survive (pp. 114–115). The test design (p. 115) matters for reading Results: 45 co-registered test locations total — 15 Taylor locations shared across all three datasets (45 test samples) for the sensor comparison, plus 30 multi-valley locations shared by both lidar epochs (60 samples) for generalization. She admits label noise where stream edges are low-relief or confused with strata/outcrops (p. 114).
**Takeaway:** ~6x more label sites, spread across valleys, epochs, and confuser landforms, with a test split explicitly built to separate "sensor effect" from "valley generalization."

### Methods › U-Net Architecture and Training (pp. 115–117)
**What's happening:** Same family as ch. 4 — U-Net with ResNet18 backbone — but the training stack moves from the DroneDeploy-derived Python workflow to ArcGIS Pro 3.3.0's Deep Learning Toolbox (PyTorch backend, RTX 3090 24 GB). She claims the new stack produced less disjointed, more continuous stream predictions (p. 115).
**Don't get lost:** Her concrete recipe is all on p. 116: 300×300 tiles, batch 32, 20% validation split, class-weighted cross-entropy for the stream/non-stream imbalance, dropout 0.1, LR 0.001, and — notably — a *fixed* 50 epochs (5–10 past the early-stopping point) so every attribute-combination experiment is trained identically. Augmentation: random crop (1.0–1.1), fixed zoom, random x/y shifts, random 90° rotations, all resized back to 300×300. Metrics are per-class precision/recall/F1 (Eqs. 6.1–6.3, pp. 116–117).
**Takeaway:** A deliberately vanilla, fixed-budget training regime — the experimental variable is the input attribute stack, not the architecture.

### Methods › Prediction and Post-Processing (p. 117)
**What's happening:** Inference runs over the full MDV extent for both lidar epochs (selected regions for REMA) at 300×300 px tiles with 75 px padding, batch 4. Raw masks then pass a geometric cleanup: morphological fill of enclosed holes <50 m², then polygon filtering that drops features with area <1000 m² and aspect ratio <5.
**Don't get lost:** The aspect ratio is computed by treating each polygon as an equivalent rectangle, solving L and W from area and perimeter (equations on p. 117) — a cheap elongation test that keeps long/narrow stream-like shapes and kills compact noise blobs. This shape-prior filter is the whole false-positive strategy; there is no probability thresholding discussion.
**Takeaway:** Post-processing is a pure geometry filter — fill small holes, keep only large, elongated polygons.

### Results (p. 118)
**What's happening:** Brief framing paragraph (p. 118): three result strands — attribute ablation, final test-set accuracy, and a description of the delivered multi-valley dataset.
**Takeaway:** Read the three sub-sections; the headline numbers live in Tables 6.3–6.6.

### Results › Stream Boundary Detection (pp. 118–120)
**What's happening:** A clean single- and multi-attribute ablation. Solo (Table 6.3, p. 119): aspect (stream F1 83.6%), elevation (83.0%), slope (81.2%) lead; profile curvature, planform curvature, and accumulation lag badly (64.9–65.6%), mostly on recall (~0.58). Combos (Table 6.4, p. 120): ESA (elevation+slope+aspect) hits 85.6% stream F1, and ESA plus any one of Pr/Pl/Acc tops out at 85.7% stream / 95.1% non-stream.
**Don't get lost:** The selection logic on p. 120 is the part worth internalizing: ESA-Pl and ESA-Acc have slightly higher precision (0.885/0.886), but she picks **ESA-Pr** because it has the highest recall (0.837 within the top tier) and yields more continuous, less fragmented channels — a recall-first choice justified by ephemeral, subtle targets. Also note the diminishing/negative returns of piling on features: ESA-PrPl-Acc drops to 84.2% stream F1.
**Takeaway:** Elevation+slope+aspect does the heavy lifting; one curvature channel adds a point; more inputs than that actively hurt.

### Results › Final Model Test Set Performance (pp. 121–123)
**What's happening:** Two evaluations of the frozen ESA-Pr model. Taylor Valley tri-sensor comparison (Table 6.5, p. 121): stream F1 of 81.7% (2001 NASA), 81.5% (2014 NCALM), 78.6% (2014 REMA); non-stream 90.2/89.8/88.4%. Multi-valley lidar comparison (Table 6.6, p. 123): NASA stream F1 81.5% (precision 87.1%), NCALM 79.8% (recall 77.4%, slightly higher than NASA's 76.5%).
**Don't get lost:** Table 6.5 (p. 121) and Table 6.6 (p. 123) carry the headlines. The qualitative failure modes on pp. 121–122 are the useful part: all sensors fragment on wide shallow low-relief channels and small streams without developed levees; REMA is worst there and has softer edges; and in places the model out-performs the manual labels, catching narrow channels the annotator missed — a label-quality caveat on the metrics themselves. Figure 6.2 (p. 122) and Figure 6.3 (p. 124) are figure-only comparison panels of predictions vs ground truth.
**Takeaway:** ~80–82% stream F1 holds up multi-valley on lidar, with REMA a few points behind and low-relief/narrow channels the shared weak spot.

### Results › Multi-Valley Stream Boundary Dataset Description (pp. 124–126)
**What's happening:** Describes the deliverable: stream boundary polygons across the northern valleys (Victoria, Barwick, McKelvey, Balham, Bull Pass, Wright, the Labyrinth), central valleys (Taylor, Pearce, Beacon), and the Denton Hills coastal valleys, for both the 2001 and 2014 lidar epochs. The northern-valley coverage alone is quoted at ~100 km² of stream area and ~3,700 km of stream length (pp. 124–125).
**Don't get lost:** Pages 125–126 are largely an inventory — named streams per region (Onyx River, Canada, Von Guerard, Delta, etc.), pointing to appendix Table C.1 for the full coverage list. Two flags worth noting: many mapped segments are *undocumented* channels (heaviest in northern valleys and Denton Hills) still needing verification as active vs ephemeral, and the polygons represent surface form at acquisition time, not flow status. Figure 6.4 (p. 126) is the figure-only full-MDV map.
**Takeaway:** The product is a multi-epoch, multi-valley polygon layer that substantially exceeds any prior centerline inventory, including many never-mapped tributaries.

### Discussion (pp. 127–129)
**What's happening:** Interprets the ablation (aspect wins solo because it flags directional inflections at channel edges even in low relief; weak features become useful only as complements), defends the recall-first ESA-Pr pick, and revisits sensor differences. Grand totals appear here (p. 128): ~110, 138, and 20 km² of stream area and ~4,550, 5,400, and 1,000 km of stream length in the 2001, 2014, and 2022 datasets respectively.
**Don't get lost:** The most interesting finding for a segmentation practitioner is on p. 128: the *coarser* 2001 lidar slightly beats the 2014 lidar in multi-valley F1, and her explanation is that the high-res 2014 data reveals tiny, weakly entrenched streams that are too thin to survive the network's early downsampling/pooling — a resolution-vs-receptive-field failure mode, not a data-quality one. REMA fragmentation in shaded, high-relief terrain is attributed to photogrammetric artifacts. Transferability claim (pp. 128–129): terrain-attribute inputs should port to other polar/arid ephemeral systems, but vegetation or urban clutter would demand local retraining.
**Takeaway:** The chapter closes with validated, MDV-wide stream masks — plus the honest caveat that higher-resolution input can *lower* measured accuracy when targets shrink below the network's effective receptive field.

## Chapter 7 — The Payoff: Two Decades of Geomorphic Change (PDF pp. 130–165)
**The chapter in one breath:** Barlow differences three DEM epochs (2001 lidar, 2014 lidar, 2021–23 REMA) inside 116 deep-learning-mapped stream boundaries to quantify erosion, deposition, and acceleration across the Dry Valleys — and finds widespread, mostly accelerating change concentrated in Denton Hills.
This is where the dissertation converges: the U-Net stream boundaries from Chapter 6 act as the stencil that isolates fluvial change, and the co-registered DEM pairs from Chapter 5 are the measuring stick. Everything before this chapter was tooling; this chapter is the science.

### Introduction (pp. 130–133)
**What's happening:** She frames the gap — long-term, valley-scale change observations in the MDVs are scarce outside a few historically monitored channels — and states the plan: quantify volumetric erosion, deposition, net and gross change for two intervals (2001–2014, 2014–2021/23) within 116 U-Net-mapped stream boundaries spanning Taylor, Wright, and Victoria Valleys, the Denton Hills (Garwood, Miers, Marshall, Ward), plus Barwick/Hidden Valley, Bull Pass, the Scott Coast, and Pearse.
**Don't get lost:** Pages 132–133 are figure-only (Fig. 7.1 study-region overview; Fig. 7.2 mapped stream locations). Stream names and IDs live in appendix Table D.1 — she refers to streams by ID throughout, so keep Fig. 7.2/Table D.1 handy.
**Takeaway:** Deep-learning boundaries let her extend change detection from a handful of monitored streams to 116 channels valley-wide.

### Methods (p. 134)
**What's happening:** One-sentence framing plus Fig. 7.3, the workflow diagram: deep-learning stream boundaries feed a co-registered multi-temporal DEM-differencing pipeline for volumetric sediment analysis.
**Don't get lost:** Fig. 7.3 (p. 134) is the whole chapter on one page — worth 30 seconds before reading the four subsections.
**Takeaway:** Stencil (Ch. 6) plus measuring stick (Ch. 5) equals sediment budgets.

#### Data Pre-Processing (pp. 134–135)
**What's happening:** Three epochs: 2001 NASA lidar (sparse, 0.14–0.32 pts/m²), 2014 NCALM lidar (2.7 pts/m²), and 2-m REMA stereo DEMs from WorldView imagery. Lidar is rasterized at 2 m (Natural Neighbor); everything is put in EPSG:3294 with EGM2008 heights; REMA was already ICP co-registered to the 2014 lidar per Chapter 5.
**Don't get lost:** Note the honest caveat on pp. 134–135: the third epoch was meant to be 2021 alone, but those DEMs lacked coverage and accuracy, so the window was widened to 2021–2023 — this is why "2021-23" appears everywhere and why she later warns about interannual melt variability. Dataset summary is back in Table 6.1 and Figure 3.5.
**Takeaway:** All three epochs end up as 2-m DEMs in one reference frame, with REMA's messiness managed rather than eliminated.

#### Stream Boundary Classification (p. 135)
**What's happening:** A recap, not new work: stream polygons for all three epochs come from the Chapter 4/6 multi-attribute U-Net (elevation, slope, aspect, profile curvature), validated against manual delineations in Chapter 6.
**Don't get lost:** Skim this — the validation numbers are deliberately not repeated here; they're in Chapter 6.
**Takeaway:** The change-detection mask is the Chapter 6 model output, applied per epoch.

#### Uncertainty Propagation (p. 136)
**What's happening:** Because vertical error varies stream to stream, she rejects a single global detection threshold. For each stream and each DEM pair, she measures elevation differences over nearby stable terrain (10–300 m buffers off the stream boundary, excluding lakes/glaciers with a 75-m buffer and manually removing obvious real change), computes NMAD and standard deviation, and sets a per-stream, per-pair Level of Detection.
**Key terms:** *NMAD* — normalized median absolute deviation, a robust standard-deviation stand-in. *LOD95* — level of detection at 95% confidence, here 1.96 × NMAD; elevation changes smaller than this are treated as noise. *DoD* — DEM of Difference.
**Don't get lost:** The math in plain terms: measure how noisy the DEM pair looks over ground that shouldn't have changed near each stream, then only believe elevation changes bigger than ~2× that noise level. Per-stream values are in appendix Table D.1.
**Takeaway:** Every volumetric number in the Results has already been filtered through a stream-specific noise threshold.

#### Change Detection (pp. 136–138)
**What's happening:** Boundaries from each epoch pair are merged (116 streams for 2001–2014: 50 named, 66 unnamed; only 38 Taylor Valley streams for 2014–2021/23, limited by REMA coverage). After LOD95 masking, she computes two rates per stream: gross (sum of |Δz|·area / Δt — total sediment reworking) and net (sum of signed Δz·area / Δt — positive = net deposition/aggradation, negative = net erosion/incision). Rates are also normalized by active channel area and scaled to a 10-year period for size-fair comparison.
**Key terms:** *Rgross vs Rnet* — magnitude of change vs erosion–deposition balance. *Sign convention* — positive Δz = deposition, negative = erosion; this holds through every table and map. *Geomorphic acceleration* — (R₂ − R₁) / (difference in epoch midpoints), in m³ yr⁻², computed only where all three epochs overlap.
**Don't get lost:** The equations on pp. 137–138 are simpler than the typesetting suggests — they are per-pixel volume sums divided by years. Note the honest subjectivity flag on p. 137: unnamed channels were segmented into units by visual interpretation ("(u)" suffix, IDs in Table D.1), with user judgment where branching forced choices.
**Takeaway:** Two numbers per stream per epoch pair — how much sediment moved, and which way the balance tipped — plus an acceleration where three epochs stack.

### Results (p. 138)
**What's happening:** A half-page roadmap: error statistics first, then per-stream volumetrics for each interval, then acceleration in the Taylor Valley three-epoch overlap.
**Don't get lost:** Nothing to study here; it just orders the next four subsections.
**Takeaway:** Read the results in the order she lists — errors, 2001–2014, 2014–2021/23, acceleration.

#### Statistical Vertical Error Analysis (p. 139)
**What's happening:** Stable-terrain checks quantify how much noisier REMA is than lidar. Lidar–lidar (2001–2014): NMAD 0.07–0.46 m, LOD95 0.15–0.92 m, STD 0.08–0.8 m. REMA-to-2014-lidar (Taylor Valley): NMAD 0.19–0.53 m, LOD95 0.37–1.04 m.
**Don't get lost:** One paragraph; the full per-stream breakdown is appendix Table D.1. The practical read: the satellite-era interval needs elevation changes roughly 0.4–1 m before they count, so its volumetrics are inherently more conservative.
**Takeaway:** Detection floors are stream-specific and roughly twice as high for the REMA interval as for lidar–lidar.

#### Volumetric Change: 2001–2014 (pp. 139–149)
**What's happening:** The core results. Denton Hills dominates: Ward Stream (ID 84) is the single most active channel — gross flux 78,713 m³ yr⁻¹, almost entirely erosion (77,598 m³ yr⁻¹ erosion vs 1,116 m³ yr⁻¹ deposition; net –76,482 m³ yr⁻¹). Garwood (83), Marshall (15), and Onyx/Wright (17) follow in gross activity (62,637; 34,915; 26,625 m³ yr⁻¹). Denton Hills streams are predominantly erosional; Onyx, Murray, Kite, Thomas, Wales, Lizotte, and Brownworth are deposition-dominated (Onyx net +13,418 m³ yr⁻¹ is the standout). Spatially: coastal Taylor Valley is depositional, central/upper Taylor and southern valley walls lean erosional, Wright/Victoria are mostly balanced-to-depositional.
**Don't get lost:** The numbers that carry the story are Ward's row and the Denton Hills group; everything else is texture. Skim strategy: read the prose on pp. 139–140 and 143–148, treat Figs. 7.5–7.6 (pp. 141–142, per-stream bar charts ranked by gross flux, figure-only pages) as reference, and give real attention to the four maps — Fig. 7.7 (p. 144, gross), Fig. 7.8 (p. 145, net), Fig. 7.9 (p. 147, area-normalized gross in m/decade), Fig. 7.10 (p. 149, area-normalized net). Normalization matters: per unit area Denton Hills still wins (Ward highest), and small streams like Joyce (u) (ID 50) jump out. Full per-stream metrics are appendix Table D.2. Two typographic snags in the prose: the Onyx/Wright ID labeling on p. 139 is muddled (both tagged ID 17), and one deposition list on pp. 139–140 has more numbers than streams — trust Table D.2 over the inline lists.
**Takeaway:** Between 2001 and 2014 the Dry Valleys were geomorphically busy, and Denton Hills — especially Ward Stream — was overwhelmingly erosional while coastal Taylor Valley quietly aggraded.

#### Volumetric Change: 2014–2021/23 (pp. 150–155)
**What's happening:** Restricted to 38 Taylor Valley streams with adequate REMA coverage. The pattern repeats: mostly moderate-to-high activity with net deposition dominant, echoing 2001–2014. Headliners: McClintock Point (ID 64) posts the region's highest net deposition (+9,403 m³ yr⁻¹ from 10,452 deposition vs 1,049 erosion); the adjacent Quinn pair are the most active — Quinn Gully West (ID 53) net +6,210, gross 11,890 m³ yr⁻¹; Quinn Valley (ID 93) largest gross flux at 11,998 m³ yr⁻¹ but smaller net (+2,657). Upper Commonwealth (ID 37, glacier side only) is near balance (net –399); an unnamed Hjorth Hill stream (ID 57) is one of the few net-erosional channels (–412.9 m³ yr⁻¹... reported as 412.9 net erosion).
**Don't get lost:** Much shorter than the 2001–2014 section — read all the prose (pp. 150, 153). Pages 151–152 and 154–155 are figure-only: Fig. 7.11 (example change-rate maps), Fig. 7.12 (per-stream flux bars), Fig. 7.13 (gross/net maps for Taylor Valley). Remember the higher LOD95 floor from p. 139 when comparing magnitudes against the lidar interval.
**Takeaway:** Taylor Valley stayed depositional into the 2020s, with the Quinn tributaries and McClintock Point emerging as the new hotspots.

#### Geomorphic Acceleration (pp. 156–161)
**What's happening:** Acceleration = change in the volumetric rate between the two intervals, divided by the years between epoch midpoints, in m³ yr⁻² — computed per stream, only where 2001, 2014, and 2021–23 coverage all overlap (Taylor Valley). Headline: the overall trend is acceleration, not slowdown. Fastest: McClintock Point (gross 798, net +777 m³ yr⁻²), Quinn Valley (gross 816, net +33), Quinn West (gross 809, net +374); several Hjorth Hill tributaries (IDs 67, 69, 63) exceed +140 m³ yr⁻² net depositional acceleration. A smaller set accelerates erosionally — Lake Joyce 1 (ID 101, –371 m³ yr⁻²) and Hjorth Hill E5 (ID 68, –158) lead. Most streams are near-balanced (<30 m³ yr⁻² net, e.g., Canada, Scar Peak, Doran, Mummy–Delfie).
**Key terms:** *Geomorphic acceleration* — not a physical acceleration of flow, but the second derivative of the sediment budget: is the annual rate of sediment movement itself increasing?
**Don't get lost:** Pages 157–158 and 160–161 are figure-only (Figs. 7.14–7.16, gross and net acceleration maps/bars for Taylor Valley). The prose on pp. 156 and 159 contains every number you need. Keep in mind her later caveat (p. 165): acceleration is computed on overlapping segments only, not whole networks.
**Takeaway:** Where three epochs stack, gross geomorphic activity is speeding up — consistent with intensifying melt-driven sediment mobilization rather than a system winding down.

### Discussion (pp. 162–165)
**What's happening:** She interprets the geography through climate zones and ground-ice: Denton Hills is most active because it sits in the Coastal Thaw Zone atop ice-cored drift, where thermokarst incision and bank collapse move large volumes; Taylor Valley's coastal deposition reflects strong glacier-to-sea hydrological connectivity plus warmer CTZ summers; southern (north-facing, equator-facing) valley walls erode more due to solar-radiation asymmetry; Wright and Victoria are quieter because they sit in the colder, drier Inland Mixed Zone with dry-frozen permafrost and weak connectivity. Against a backdrop of the MDVs being "relatively stable until recent decades," the patterns point to increasing melt and permafrost destabilization.
**Don't get lost:** The limitations paragraphs (pp. 164–165) are the most important part for a practitioner: (1) 2001 coverage gaps exclude upstream coastal Taylor reaches, so coastal gross rates and the erosional component are likely underestimated; (2) stereo REMA error is higher and terrain-dependent — residual misalignment on steep slopes can fake lateral erosion/deposition, and poor viewing geometry can mimic infilling in narrow incised channels; (3) the multi-year 2021–23 window means interannual melt variability may skew results; (4) stable-terrain masks may include subtle real change, biasing LOD thresholds high — meaning the whole analysis errs toward under-detection, not overstatement. Future-work list (p. 165): keep monitoring Denton Hills, use consistent high-res sensors, automate REMA co-registration, and build terrain-aware REMA uncertainty models.
**Takeaway:** The Dry Valleys' reputation for stability no longer holds — and because every methodological bias here pushes toward conservatism, the reported change is a floor, not a ceiling.

## Chapter 8 — Conclusions & Future Directions (PDF pp. 166–171)
**The chapter in one breath:** Barlow closes by restating what the dissertation delivered — a semi-automated, multi-valley method for detecting stream boundaries from elevation derivatives alone and isolating fluvial geomorphic change in the MDVs — then lays out four future-work directions and a short reflection on why monitoring Earth's most stable landscapes matters.

### Chapter opening — what the research delivered (pp. 166–167)
**What's happening:** Two pages of synthesis, no new results. The contributions in order: deep-learning-segmented stream boundaries used to isolate fluvial geomorphic change at multi-valley scale; a more complete, up-to-date stream inventory for the MDVs including newly identified channels; and the demonstration that detection works from high-resolution elevation-model derivatives rather than RGB — a real advantage where streams are usually dry (p166). She contrasts this with prior work that examined channels one at a time.
**Don't get lost:** The "elevation derivatives, not RGB" point on p166 is the methodological headline — it's what makes the approach portable to desiccated or data-poor regions. The framing sentence about identifying "regions of accelerating fluvial geomorphic change at a broader scale" (p166, bottom) is the thesis in miniature.
**Takeaway:** The dissertation's core claim is a scalable, imagery-independent way to map and monitor dry-stream change across whole valleys, not channel-by-channel.

### Future Work 1 — Long-Term Monitoring of Fluvial Geomorphic Change (pp. 167–168)
**What's happening:** The study covered 2001–2014 and 2014–2021/23 using aerial lidar plus REMA stereographic DEMs, and REMA was validated as a repeatable dataset — so the obvious extension is more valleys and longer records. That means more co-registration, cleaning, QC, and error analysis of REMA data (p168), ideally automated via ML to cut manual workload. She flags Denton Hills as the priority area (more pronounced change) and recommends grant-funded UAV acquisition to supplement terrestrial/airborne lidar in sensitive zones, plus monitoring at seasonal/annual/decadal scales.
**Don't get lost:** The Denton Hills recommendation and the UAV-grant suggestion are both on p168; the multi-temporal-scale point closes the section.
**Takeaway:** REMA-based monitoring is proven and ready to scale across valleys — the bottleneck is co-registration/cleaning labor, and Denton Hills should go first.

### Future Work 2 — Expanding Detection to Meltwater and Glacier Surfaces (p. 169)
**What's happening:** Prior MDV change detection on glaciers and lakes lacked automated feature detection; the stream-boundary method could track accumulating meltwater, new ponds, and standing water — early-warning signs of permafrost degradation. Notably, preliminary runs with only a few glacial training examples already picked out supraglacial streams and crevasses (a result not shown in Chapters 4 or 6), suggesting the segmentation transfers to glacier surface features relevant to mass balance, melt rates, and flow acceleration.
**Don't get lost:** The buried gem is the "although not discussed in Chapter 4 or 6" sentence mid-p169 — an unpublished preliminary result. Citations here (Höfle 2007 on crevasses; Hoffman 2008, Bergstrom 2021 on supraglacial melt) anchor the glaciology framing.
**Takeaway:** The model already shows unplanned skill at glacial surface features, making meltwater/glacier mapping the lowest-hanging extension.

### Future Work 3 — Climate Variable Correlation for Predictive Modeling (p. 170)
**What's happening:** The change-detection and acceleration estimates can feed predictive models: flagging reaches prone to avulsion, deepening/widening, and erosion/deposition; reading sediment-flux fluctuations as permafrost-stability indicators; and using stream-activity increases to infer active deglaciation, including subsurface melt driven by supraglacial water. She also pitches the interdisciplinary angle — feeding outputs into distributed hydrological and nutrient-flux models to prioritize conservation of microbial ecosystems.
**Don't get lost:** The logical chain on p170 runs geomorphic acceleration → permafrost/deglaciation inference → hydrologic/ecologic modeling — read it as an argument for why the change maps are inputs, not endpoints.
**Takeaway:** The dissertation's change products are positioned as the raw material for climate-linked predictive and ecological modeling.

### Future Work 4 — Model Performance and Training Set Diversification (p. 171)
**What's happening:** Two tracks: within the MDVs, add training samples (more REMA and terrestrial data); globally, build toward a generalizable stream-boundary model by adding sensor-diverse inputs at varied resolutions across tropical/temperate, high/low relief, and stream-to-river scales. Fusing stereo DEMs, radar, thermal, and hyperspectral data plus derived indices would reduce uncertainty where single-source segmentation fails.
**Don't get lost:** Short section — the multi-sensor fusion list (p171) is the concrete shopping list for a generalized model.
**Takeaway:** The path from an MDV-tuned model to a global one runs through more diverse labels and multi-sensor input stacks.

### Reflections and Broader Implications (pp. 171–172)
**What's happening:** The MDVs matter precisely because of their historic geomorphic and climatic stability — documenting change there is compelling evidence for policymakers that climate change reaches even Earth's most stable places. She closes by arguing that large-scale automation in mapping and modeling must be prioritized to identify at-risk regions and model climate-trajectory scenarios, and frames this work as the foundation for large-scale polar fluvial monitoring.
