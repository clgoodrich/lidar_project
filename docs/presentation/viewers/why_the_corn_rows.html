Why the corn rows are where they are

The laser never covers the ground evenly. It rules lines on it, about a metre apart. Whether you ever see those lines depends on where you are standing in the swath — and the reason is the shape of the mirror's swing.

2   What the slider is actually showing

Watch the two arms of each V as you move outward.

Straight below the plane, the mirror is at full speed. The beam crosses you on the way out and on the way back at evenly spaced moments, so the lines land 1.03 m, 1.03 m, 1.03 m.

Near the edge of the swing, the mirror has to slow, stop and reverse. The there-line and the back-line now cross you almost at the same instant, so they land nearly on top of each other — and then nothing arrives until the next full swing. The gaps stop being even and become tiny, huge, tiny, huge.

The widest gap runs from 1.03 m directly below the plane to 2.07 m at the edge of the swing. It exactly doubles. That is the whole mechanism, and it needs no assumption about flying height — only the mirror rate and the airspeed, both of which are in the file.

The rise is the gap doubling. The cliff is the next flight line arriving: inside 14°, 0% of the ground is seen by a second pass; beyond it, 98.3% is. A second pass rules its own lines in different places, so it fills the first one's gaps.

Share of cells with no return. Single-coverage ground only, so the flight-line overlap cannot flatter any column.

Read down a column: trees roughly double it. Read across a row: the angle multiplies it thirty-fold. Open ground straight below the plane is 0.16%. Tall trees two thirds of the way out is 5.43%.

So a corn row needs the recipe: the middle-to-outer part of a single pass, before the neighbouring strip starts helping, preferably under trees. Everywhere else the lines are still being ruled — you just cannot see them, because something filled them in.

What here is measured, and what is drawn

Worth keeping straight.

Measured from the delivered point cloud: mirror rate 54.3 Hz, from 362 direction reversals in 3.383 s of GPS time. Airspeed 112.2 m/s, regressed from position against GPS time. Pulse rate 336 kHz. Swath out to 19.9°. Every percentage in sections 3 and 4.

Drawn from those numbers: the beam track in section 1 is a sine of wavelength 112.2 / 54.3 = 2.07 m. The crossing positions and the gaps between them follow from it with no free parameters.

Simplified: the second flight line is drawn at the angle it would really be at for this ground, but with an arbitrary phase — nothing fixes where its mirror happened to be. And a gap does not become a hole the moment it opens; the gridder bridges narrow ones, which is why the measured curve in section 3 is not simply the gap width.
